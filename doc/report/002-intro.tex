\section{Introduction}

The use of Artificial Intelligence (AI) in healthcare environment
presents exceptional opportunities to optimize processes, improve
diagnosis precision and relieve healthcare staff from executing
repetitive tasks to provide a better treatment to patients. Unlike other
machine learning applications, medical data cannot be easily
centralized for training models, as strict privacy policies are
imposed to protect the patient. Furthermore, clinical information is
inherently distributed across multiple distinct hospitals and
organizations, and the datasets themselves are frequently unbalanced
due to the scarcity of specific diagnostic classes or rare diseases.

This mini-project illustrates these core challenges and proposes a
modular, end-to-end AI pipeline designed to overcome them. To validate
the architecture, we selected the binary classification of Chest
X-Rays (Pneumonia vs. Normal). While modern Convolutional Neural
Networks (CNNs) can easily achieve high accuracy on this specific
task, the dataset presents a significant class imbalance due to a
scarcity of the minority 'NORMAL' class, making it an ideal candidate
for our project.

The true goal of this report is to demonstrate a framework that
navigates privacy, distribution, and data scarcity. To achieve this,
the proposed system architecture leverages three distinct
techniques: 
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Generative Adversarial Networks (GAN):} A Deep
  Convolutional GAN (DCGAN) is implemented to generate realistic
  synthetic images and augment the minority class ('NORMAL' class in
  our use case).
  
\item \textbf{Convolutional Neural Networks (CNN):} Performs the
  binary diagnostic classification (Pneumonia vs. Normal).
  
\item \textbf{Federated Learning (FL)}: Enables model training across
  simulated hospital clients without pooling data. Only model weights
  are shared centrally via \texttt{FedAvg}, keeping raw data local.
\end{itemize}

The report is organized as follows: Section II
details the system architecture, including the GAN, CNN, and Federated
Learning configurations. Section III presents the comparative results
between centralized and federated training. Finally, Section IV
outlines the main conclusions.
